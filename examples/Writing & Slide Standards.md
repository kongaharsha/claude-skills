# Writing & Slide Standards
*Last updated: 2026-04-10*

## Core Principle

This project is about decision support, not page production. Outputs should help leadership understand what matters, why it matters, and what decision or action should follow.

## Slide Brainstorming Rules

- Never build PowerPoint slides directly.
- Never jump straight into drafting slides without aligning on the storyline.
- For any deck request, first align on:
  - the audience
  - the objective or decision to support
  - the 3-5 messages that matter most
  - the minimum slide sequence needed to land those messages
- For each slide, align on:
  - the single point of the slide (one message per page)
  - the lede (an assertion, not a label)
  - the best page structure for that point
  - the evidence, chart, framework, or table that belongs on the page
  - the implication the reader should take away
- Do not assume a fixed slide format. Choose page structure based on the point being made.
- Always respond inline in chat so the user can create the actual slide.
- If a proposed slide is descriptive rather than decision-useful, challenge it.

## Lede Standards

- A lede should be an assertion, not a label.
- It should make clear what changed, how much, and why it matters.
- Avoid generic titles like "Market Overview", "Competitive Landscape", or "Financial Summary".
- Prefer ledes that answer why the point matters now.

**Weak:** "Market sizing overview"
**Strong:** "Mid-market healthcare IT is a $35-45B opportunity growing at 18% — but only ~$8B is realistically addressable given our capabilities"

## Storyline Patterns

- situation → implication → options → recommendation
- market change → risk to us → evidence → choices
- capability gap → why it matters → what to do
- performance change → diagnosis → future risk → intervention options

## Analysis & Visualization

- Share insights in chat before creating files — especially in early analysis
- Start with the business question the analysis is trying to answer
- Lead with the answer and why it matters
- Use rounded, executive-readable numbers by default
- Label source files and sheets for important metrics
- If a metric is approximate or based on a proxy, say so explicitly
- When recommending a chart, specify the type and explain why
- Provide copy-paste-ready data tables when useful
- Prefer simple, decision-useful visuals over diagnostic clutter

## Writing Standards

- Lead with the implication, not the process
- Write for a time-constrained executive reader
- Be concise, specific, and directional
- Challenge weak framing and weak logic, not just weak wording
- Favor a clear point of view over a neutral summary when the evidence supports it

## What To Avoid

- Descriptive slides with no point of view
- Raw table dumps as the main output
- Overstuffed pages
- Vague titles
- Unrounded numbers with no decision value
- Laundry-list competitor pages with no strategic implication
- Presenting assumptions as confirmed fact
